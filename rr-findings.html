Long-term panel: from “× WHO” to relative risk

What the Long-term panel shows today, what WHO and COMEAP recommend for comparing long-term exposure across pollutants, and how each pollutant’s bar would change. All patient values are the prototype mock patient unless a monitoring site is named.

In one minute
  • The Long-term panel shows each pollutant as a multiple of its WHO guideline (3.6×, 1.9×…). A multiple is an exceedance signal, not a health scale: the guidelines sit at very different concentrations and each pollutant has a different strength of link to mortality.
  • WHO 2021 and COMEAP express long-term health effects as a relative risk per 10 µg/m³ (the concentration–response function, CRF). Applying each pollutant’s own CRF gives % higher long-term mortality than in air at the WHO guideline, which can be compared pollutant by pollutant.
  • On that scale PM₂.₅ leads: the mock patient’s 3.6× becomes about +11%; NO₂’s 2.2× becomes about +3%.
  • Ozone is the biggest problem. We divide an annual mean by a peak-season guideline, which understates it (Honor Oak Park reads 0.77× WHO; the station’s peak season is 1.42×), and its long-term evidence is the weakest of the four.
  • PM₁₀ contains PM₂.₅, so it stays as a bar against WHO, split into fine and coarse, without its own percentage. Each percentage stands on its own; they describe overlapping air, so they are read separately rather than summed.

Overview
1 · Current approach

What we do now

For each pollutant we take the exposure service’s annual mean at the patient’s address and divide it by the WHO guideline. The bar fills to the dotted WHO line, then the excess above the line is scaled so the pollutant with the largest multiple fills the top.

ratio = annual mean ÷ WHO guideline → label “3.6×” above-line height = (ratio − 1) ÷ largest (ratio − 1) across the four pollutants
Current Long-term panel (Design 3.2b) · mock patient

Why a multiple is not enough

  1. The same multiple means very different amounts of pollution. 1.3× WHO is an extra 1.5 µg/m³ of PM₂.₅ but an extra 18 µg/m³ of ozone.
  2. Each pollutant has a different steepness of risk. Per microgram, PM₂.₅ is about three times as steep as NO₂ and eight times as steep as ozone. The multiple ignores this.
  3. Ozone is compared on the wrong metric. The WHO ozone figure (60) is a peak-season value; the exposure service returns an annual mean of all hours, which runs about 1.5× lower.
  4. A row of similar bars invites adding them up. PM₂.₅, PM₁₀ and NO₂ largely describe the same traffic-related air, so a combined reading overstates the total.
2 · Evidence

What WHO and the research recommend

WHO and the UK Committee on the Medical Effects of Air Pollutants (COMEAP) do not use multiples of the guideline. They quantify long-term effects with a concentration–response function (CRF), usually quoted as the relative risk (RR) per 10 µg/m³.

RR and CRF in one paragraph

The CRF is the rule linking concentration to risk across the whole range: its shape and its steepness. The RR is one reading from that rule: how much higher the risk is at one concentration than at another. For long-term mortality, WHO and COMEAP use a log-linear CRF, so each extra 10 µg/m³ multiplies risk by the same factor. That shape is described by one number, which is why the reports quote “RR 1.08 per 10 µg/m³” for PM₂.₅. (Cohort studies report hazard ratios; for this purpose they mean the same thing.)

RR(Δc) = RR₁₀ ^ (Δc ÷ 10) Δc = annual mean − WHO guideline % higher risk = (RR − 1) × 100 Worked example · PM₂.₅ 9.6 µg/m³: Δc = 4.6 → 1.08^0.46 = 1.036 → about 3.6% higher

Rules that come with the numbers

  • Compare with the WHO guideline, not with zero. “+10.7%” means higher than in air at the guideline, which keeps the panel anchored to WHO.
  • Show each pollutant on its own. COMEAP treats PM₂.₅ as the indicator of the outdoor mixture; NO₂ describes much of the same air and PM₁₀ contains PM₂.₅. Summing the percentages counts the same air more than once.
  • Long-term coefficients only. Short-term (daily) coefficients belong to the Recent and Today panels; see section 8.
  • Population risk, not personal risk. These are relative risks across a population living with this air. The absolute change depends on the patient’s baseline: +10.7% moves a 2% annual death risk (an older patient) to about 2.2%, and a healthy 30-year-old’s 0.1% to about 0.11%.
  • Linear, no cut-off. Both WHO and COMEAP calculate log-linearly and continue below the guideline; both note the true curve may be steeper near clean air (see PM₂.₅).

“If mortality effects estimated using this coefficient are added to estimates of mortality effects associated with other pollutants, this will likely give an overestimate of the effects of the pollution mix.”

COMEAP summary of quantification recommendations · PM₂.₅ long-term

“The results should not be added together as this would lead to an over-estimation of the effects.”

COMEAP 2018 NO₂ summary · on PM₂.₅- and NO₂-based estimates

4 · Biggest problem

Ozone (O₃)

Problem 1: an annual mean against a peak-season guideline

WHO’s long-term ozone guideline is the average of the daily maximum 8-hour mean over the six consecutive months with the highest ozone. In the UK that is the warm season; we use a fixed April–September. The exposure service’s value is an annual mean of all hours, which includes the low winter and night-time values, so dividing it by 60 understates ozone against WHO.

Problem 2: the weakest long-term evidence

WHO’s long-term ozone CRF is built on peak-season exposure; studies using an annual-mean metric found no association with all-cause mortality. That is a second reason the annual mean is the wrong input. A 2024 update of the WHO review widened the peak-season interval to include no effect and rated it low certainty.

“The peak season is defined as the six consecutive months of the year with the highest six-month running-average ozone concentration.”

WHO AQG 2021, ch. 3 · ozone, peak season

“RR = 1.01 (95% CI: 1.00–1.02) per 10 µg/m3 increase in peak-season average of daily maximum 8-hour mean ozone concentrations, assuming a linear relationship … The certainty of the evidence was considered moderate according to GRADE.”

WHO AQG 2021, ch. 3 · ozone, long-term

“Studies of O3 using annual metrics showed the associations with all-cause and respiratory mortality were 0.97 (0.93, 1.02) and 0.99 (0.89, 1.11) per 10 μg/m3 respectively. For studies using peak O3 metrics, the association with all-cause mortality was 1.01 (1.00, 1.02).”

Huangfu & Atkinson 2020 · abstract

“A systematic review and quantitative assessment of the evidence from cohort studies suggested no association between long-term annual ozone concentrations and mortality … and therefore quantification is not recommended.”

COMEAP 2015 ozone report · Q9

How the ozone bar changes

  • Input: a peak-season value. Either from the hourly nowcast (about 26 weekly /coords requests per address for one April–September), or, better, a peak-season map layer from the exposure service.
  • Label: hatched and marked “moderate certainty”, the weakest of the long-term estimates.
  • Until a peak-season value exists: the current bar understates ozone. Options are to hide it, grey it out, or keep it with a caveat (see decisions).

Is the curve linear?

Under the method WHO and COMEAP use, almost exactly: +5 µg/m³ above the guideline ≈ +3.9%, +10 ≈ +8.0%, +20 ≈ +16.6%. Both bodies note the evidence leans supralinear (steeper per microgram near clean air), and neither has adopted a non-linear curve. If the supralinear reading is right, the official method slightly understates the difference between “just over the guideline” and “at the guideline”, which is where most UK addresses sit.

“RR of 1.08 (95% CI: 1.06–1.09) per 10 µg/m3 PM2.5, assuming a linear relationship. The authors found an indication of a supralinear relationship, suggesting a steeper risk increase at lower exposure levels.”

WHO AQG 2021, ch. 3 · PM₂.₅ long-term

“Meta analyses of studies conducted at the low mean PM2.5 levels (<25, 20, 15, 12, 10 µg/m3) yielded RRs that were similar or higher compared to the overall RR, consistent with the finding of generally linear or supra-linear CRFs in individual studies.”

Chen & Hoek 2020 · abstract

“The exposure-response function might be supra-linear (that is, with a bigger effect, per unit change in concentration, at lower exposures than higher exposures). However, it is not clear to what extent these results may be due to differences in populations and/or the statistical methods used. Therefore we do not consider the evidence sufficient, at this time, to recommend any change from the current assumption of a linear CRF.”

COMEAP 2022 PM₂.₅ statement · ¶37

“We do not recommend the use of a cut-off value for quantification. We suggest quantification to zero PM2.5, using an assumption of continuing linearity.”

COMEAP 2022 PM₂.₅ statement · recommendations

How the PM₁₀ bar changes

  • Keep PM₁₀ on the panel: clinicians and regulators expect it, and the coarse share matters for respiratory patients.
  • The coarse share’s relevance is short-term (airways, asthma and COPD flare-ups), which the Recent and Today panels cover.
7 · Overlaps the traffic mixture

NO₂

A moderate long-term association that largely describes the same traffic air as PM₂.₅. It reads as more serious than it is under “× WHO” (2.2×, above PM₁₀) because its guideline is low and its CRF is shallow. The main open question is which coefficient to use.

Which coefficient?

COMEAP’s unadjusted 1.023 describes NO₂ as a marker of the traffic mixture. COMEAP judged that only 25–55% of it is NO₂ itself, giving 1.006–1.013 for policies that cut NO₂ alone. A 2024 update of the WHO review found a larger pooled RR of 1.05. The choice moves the mock patient between about +0.7% and +6%.

“RR = 1.02 (95% CI: 1.01–1.04) per 10 µg/m3 nitrogen dioxide, assuming a linear relationship. The certainty of the evidence was considered moderate according to GRADE. The authors found an indication of a supralinear relationship.”

WHO AQG 2021, ch. 3 · NO₂ long-term

“A majority view of the Committee was that approximately 25-55% of the unadjusted summary coefficient derived from the meta-analysis of single pollutant models (1.023 per 10 µg/m3 NO2) could be regarded as causally related to NO2. This gives a range of between 1.006 per 10 µg/m3 and 1.013 per 10 µg/m3.”

COMEAP 2018 NO₂ report

How the NO₂ bar changes

NO₂’s short-term effects on asthma are well established; that is what the Recent and Today panels show with DAQI. The Long-term bar stays on the long-term coefficient.

8 · Which panel each coefficient belongs to

Long-term vs short-term

The Long-term panel uses long-term (annual or peak-season) coefficients only. Short-term coefficients are much smaller per day and describe a different question, so they stay with the Recent and Today panels and the DAQI scale.

9 · For discussion

Decisions needed

  1. Panel format. Option A (risk scale), Option B (× WHO with a risk label), or lead with µg/m³ and keep relative risk on this page only?
  2. Ozone input. Hourly nowcast per patient (about 26 requests per address), or ask for a peak-season map layer from the exposure service?
  3. Ozone until then. Hide, grey out, or keep the current bar with a caveat?
  4. PM₁₀. Split fine/coarse bar as shown, or a note under PM₂.₅?
  5. NO₂ coefficient. COMEAP unadjusted 1.023 (traffic mixture), WHO 1.02, COMEAP NO₂-alone 1.006–1.013, or the 2024 update’s 1.05?
  6. Absolute risk. Is a clinician-facing example by age band wanted, or does that go beyond a population relative risk?

Wording for any clinical copy: “higher long-term risk across a population living with this air”, rather than “your risk”.
